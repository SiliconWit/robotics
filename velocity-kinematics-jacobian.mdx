Ask a robot arm to move its hand in a slow, straight line while the arm is almost fully stretched, and a joint may have to spin faster than its motor can turn. The hand leaves the line, or the robot stops with a fault. Next to a person, the same poses can turn a small motor torque into a hard push. One matrix, the Jacobian, explains both. It converts joint speeds into the velocity of the hand and hand forces into joint torques, and it shows the poses where the arm loses a direction of motion. #robotics #jacobian #velocity-kinematics

## Real-World Engineering Challenge: Collaborative Robot with Force-Limited Operation

<InArticleAd />


A collaborative robot works beside people with no safety cage. On a production line, one must hand parts to a human worker. The technical specification ISO/TS 15066 gives the largest force a robot may put on each part of the human body. In this lesson we take one limit for the whole task: 150 N at the end-effector, the tool at the end of the arm.

The motors act at the joints, but the limit is at the hand. The controller must know, at every moment, how joint speeds and joint torques appear at the hand, and in which poses a small torque gives a large force.

## Fundamental Theory

<InArticleAd />


### What is the Jacobian?

Turn one joint of an arm a little, and the hand moves a little. How far, and in which direction, depends on the pose of the arm. The Jacobian is the table that holds these answers: one column for each joint, and in it the motion of the hand for one unit of motion of that joint.

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Bicycle Gear Ratio" icon="seti:gear">
    On a bicycle, the gear ratio tells you how fast the wheel turns for a given speed of the pedals. The Jacobian does the same work for a robot: it tells you how fast the hand moves for given speeds of the joints.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Where the Comparison Stops" icon="pencil">
    A gear ratio is one fixed number. The Jacobian is a table of numbers, because there are several joints and the hand moves in several directions. And it is not fixed: every entry changes as the arm changes its pose. In some poses a whole direction of motion disappears.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### Mathematical Definition

Write the joint angles (radians) as a list $\mathbf{q} = (q_1, \dots, q_n)$, and the position of the end-effector as a list $\mathbf{x}$ of $m$ coordinates (metres). Forward kinematics is the rule $\mathbf{x} = f(\mathbf{q})$. The symbol $\partial f_1 / \partial q_2$ means "the change of the first coordinate for a small change of joint 2, with the other joints held still". The Jacobian collects all such rates:

$$
J(\mathbf{q}) = \frac{\partial f(\mathbf{q})}{\partial \mathbf{q}} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial q_1} & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial q_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial q_n} \\ \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial q_1} & \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial q_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial q_n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial q_1} & \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial q_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial q_n} \end{bmatrix}
$$

A dot over a symbol means its rate of change with time. So $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ is the list of joint speeds, in rad/s, and $\dot{\mathbf{x}}$ is the velocity of the end-effector, in m/s. Each joint adds its own share to the motion of the hand, and the shares add:

$$
\dot{\mathbf{x}} = J(\mathbf{q}) \, \dot{\mathbf{q}}
$$

In words: the velocity of the hand is the Jacobian times the joint speeds. The writing $J(\mathbf{q})$ is a reminder that the entries of $J$ depend on the pose.

:::note[Jacobian Dimensions]
$J$ has one row for each coordinate of the task and one column for each joint. For a planar 2R arm that controls a position $(x, y)$, $J$ is $2 \times 2$. For a 6-joint arm that controls position and tool direction in 3D, $J$ is $6 \times 6$.
:::

<Card title="Body vs Spatial Jacobian: Two Ways to Express Velocity" icon="information">
For later work in 3D: the velocity of the tool can be written in the frame of the robot base (the **spatial** Jacobian) or of the tool (the **body** Jacobian). Mixing them causes sign errors. In the planar examples here the choice makes no difference.
</Card>

### Full Derivation: 2-Link Planar Robot (2R)

Now build the Jacobian for a planar 2R arm with link lengths $L_1$ and $L_2$ (metres) and joint angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$.

#### Step 1: Forward Kinematics Equations

The position $(x, y)$ of the end-effector, from [Forward and Inverse Kinematics](/education/robotics/forward-inverse-kinematics/), is:

$$
x = L_1 \cos\theta_1 + L_2 \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2)
$$

$$
y = L_1 \sin\theta_1 + L_2 \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)
$$

Here $\theta_1$ is measured counterclockwise from the $x$ axis, and $\theta_2$ is the turn of link 2 away from the direction of link 1.

#### Step 2: Compute Partial Derivatives

The $2 \times 2$ Jacobian needs four rates of change: each of $x$ and $y$, for a small change of each of $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$. Use the rules that the derivative of $\cos u$ is $-\sin u$ and the derivative of $\sin u$ is $\cos u$.

**Partial derivatives of $x$:**

$$
\frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta_1} = -L_1 \sin\theta_1 - L_2 \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)
$$

$$
\frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta_2} = -L_2 \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)
$$

**Partial derivatives of $y$:**

$$
\frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta_1} = L_1 \cos\theta_1 + L_2 \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2)
$$

$$
\frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta_2} = L_2 \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2)
$$

<details>
<summary>**Detailed differentiation walkthrough**</summary>

Take $x = L_1 \cos\theta_1 + L_2 \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$. For a change of $\theta_1$: the first term gives $-L_1 \sin\theta_1$, and the second gives $-L_2 \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$, because $\theta_1$ is inside $(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$. For a change of $\theta_2$: the first term has no $\theta_2$ in it and gives zero, and the second gives $-L_2 \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$. The $y$ equation follows the same way.

</details>

#### Step 3: Assemble the Jacobian Matrix

Combining all four partial derivatives into matrix form:

$$
J(\theta_1, \theta_2) = \begin{bmatrix} -L_1 \sin\theta_1 - L_2 \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2) & -L_2 \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2) \\ L_1 \cos\theta_1 + L_2 \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) & L_2 \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) \end{bmatrix}
$$

To write less, let $s_1 = \sin\theta_1$, $c_1 = \cos\theta_1$, $s_{12} = \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$ and $c_{12} = \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$:

$$
J = \begin{bmatrix} -L_1 s_1 - L_2 s_{12} & -L_2 s_{12} \\ L_1 c_1 + L_2 c_{12} & L_2 c_{12} \end{bmatrix}
$$

The velocity $(\dot{x}, \dot{y})$ of the end-effector is then:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \dot{x} \\ \dot{y} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -L_1 s_1 - L_2 s_{12} & -L_2 s_{12} \\ L_1 c_1 + L_2 c_{12} & L_2 c_{12} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \dot{\theta}_1 \\ \dot{\theta}_2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Column 1 is the velocity of the hand when joint 1 alone turns at 1 rad/s: the whole arm swings about the base. Column 2 is the velocity of the hand when joint 2 alone turns at 1 rad/s: only link 2 swings, about the elbow. The real velocity is column 1 times $\dot{\theta}_1$ plus column 2 times $\dot{\theta}_2$.

### Forward and Inverse Velocity Mapping

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Forward Velocity Mapping">

**Problem:** The joint speeds $\dot{\theta}_1$ and $\dot{\theta}_2$ are known. Find the velocity $(\dot{x}, \dot{y})$ of the end-effector.

**Method:** Multiply.

$$
\dot{\mathbf{x}} = J(\mathbf{q}) \, \dot{\mathbf{q}}
$$

This always has an answer, in every pose.

**When to use:** To find the real velocity of the end-effector from the joint speeds that the encoders (the angle sensors on the joints) measure. A collaborative robot uses it to check that the hand is not moving too fast near a person.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Inverse Velocity Mapping">

**Problem:** The wanted velocity $(\dot{x}, \dot{y})$ of the end-effector is known. Find the joint speeds $\dot{\theta}_1$ and $\dot{\theta}_2$ that give it.

**Method:** Undo the multiplication with the inverse matrix $J^{-1}$.

$$
\dot{\mathbf{q}} = J^{-1}(\mathbf{q}) \, \dot{\mathbf{x}}
$$

For the 2R arm, the inverse of the $2 \times 2$ Jacobian is:

$$
J^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(J)} \begin{bmatrix} L_2 c_{12} & L_2 s_{12} \\ -L_1 c_1 - L_2 c_{12} & -L_1 s_1 - L_2 s_{12} \end{bmatrix}
$$

Here $\det(J)$ is the determinant of $J$, one number made from its four entries: the product of the two entries on the main diagonal minus the product of the other two.

**When to use:** To follow a path. You give the path of the hand (a straight line, for example), and at each time step this gives the joint speeds to command.

:::note[Critical Warning]
The inverse divides by $\det(J)$, so it exists only when $\det(J) \neq 0$. A pose with $\det(J) = 0$ is called a singularity. There, some velocities of the hand cannot be produced by any joint speeds.
:::

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

### Numerical Example

Take a 2R arm with $L_1 = 1.0$ m and $L_2 = 0.8$ m, in the pose $\theta_1 = 45°$, $\theta_2 = 30°$. The joints turn at $\dot{\theta}_1 = 2.0$ rad/s and $\dot{\theta}_2 = -1.0$ rad/s.

<Steps>

1. **Compute trigonometric values:**

   $\theta_1 = 45° = \pi/4$, so $s_1 = 0.7071$, $c_1 = 0.7071$

   $\theta_1 + \theta_2 = 75°$, so $s_{12} = 0.9659$, $c_{12} = 0.2588$

2. **Build the Jacobian:**

   $$
   J = \begin{bmatrix} -1.0(0.7071) - 0.8(0.9659) & -0.8(0.9659) \\ 1.0(0.7071) + 0.8(0.2588) & 0.8(0.2588) \end{bmatrix}
   $$

   $$
   J = \begin{bmatrix} -1.4798 & -0.7727 \\ 0.9142 & 0.2071 \end{bmatrix}
   $$

3. **Forward velocity mapping:**

   $$
   \begin{bmatrix} \dot{x} \\ \dot{y} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1.4798 & -0.7727 \\ 0.9142 & 0.2071 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2.0 \\ -1.0 \end{bmatrix}
   $$

   $$
   \dot{x} = (-1.4798)(2.0) + (-0.7727)(-1.0) = -2.9596 + 0.7727 = -2.187 \text{ m/s}
   $$

   $$
   \dot{y} = (0.9142)(2.0) + (0.2071)(-1.0) = 1.8284 - 0.2071 = 1.621 \text{ m/s}
   $$

   The end-effector speed is $\|\dot{\mathbf{x}}\| = \sqrt{(-2.187)^2 + (1.621)^2} = 2.72$ m/s.

4. **Compute the determinant:**

   $$
   \det(J) = (-1.4798)(0.2071) - (-0.7727)(0.9142)
   $$

   $$
   \det(J) = -0.3065 + 0.7064 \approx 0.4000
   $$

   The exact value follows from the closed form $\det(J) = L_1 L_2 \sin\theta_2 = (1.0)(0.8)(0.5) = 0.4000$. The four-digit entries above give $0.3999$; the difference is rounding only.

   Since $\det(J) \neq 0$, the robot is not at a singularity and the inverse velocity mapping is well-defined.

5. **Inverse velocity example:** Suppose we want $\dot{x} = 0.5$ m/s, $\dot{y} = 0.0$ m/s (pure horizontal motion). Then:

   $$
   \begin{bmatrix} \dot{\theta}_1 \\ \dot{\theta}_2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{0.4000} \begin{bmatrix} 0.2071 & 0.7727 \\ -0.9142 & -1.4798 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0.0 \end{bmatrix}
   $$

   $$
   \dot{\theta}_1 = \frac{(0.2071)(0.5)}{0.4000} = 0.259 \text{ rad/s}
   $$

   $$
   \dot{\theta}_2 = \frac{(-0.9142)(0.5)}{0.4000} = -1.143 \text{ rad/s}
   $$

</Steps>

:::note[Physical Interpretation]
To move the end-effector to the right at 0.5 m/s from this pose, joint 1 turns slowly counterclockwise (0.259 rad/s) and joint 2 turns faster clockwise (-1.143 rad/s). In another pose the same motion needs other joint speeds.
:::

### Singularity Analysis

Stretch your own arm out fully and try to move your fingertip further away: you cannot, however hard your shoulder and elbow work. A singularity of a robot is the same thing: a pose in which the two columns of $J$ point along one line, so the joints can move the hand along that line only. The other direction is lost, at any joint speed.

#### Determinant of the Jacobian

The determinant measures how far the two columns are from lying on one line, and it is zero when they do. For the 2R arm:

$$
\det(J) = (-L_1 s_1 - L_2 s_{12})(L_2 c_{12}) - (-L_2 s_{12})(L_1 c_1 + L_2 c_{12})
$$

Multiplying out, the terms with $L_2^2$ cancel and a sine identity leaves one short result (the steps follow below):

$$
\boxed{\det(J) = L_1 L_2 \sin\theta_2}
$$

Only the elbow angle $\theta_2$ appears. Turning joint 1 turns the whole arm and does not change how close it is to a singularity.

<details>
<summary>**Step-by-step determinant simplification**</summary>

Starting from the expanded determinant:
$\det(J) = -L_1 L_2 s_1 c_{12} - L_2^2 s_{12} c_{12} + L_1 L_2 s_{12} c_1 + L_2^2 s_{12} c_{12}$

The $L_2^2$ terms cancel: $\det(J) = L_1 L_2 (s_{12} c_1 - s_1 c_{12})$

Now apply the subtraction formula: $\sin(\alpha - \beta) = \sin\alpha \cos\beta - \cos\alpha \sin\beta$

With $\alpha = \theta_1 + \theta_2$ and $\beta = \theta_1$: $s_{12} c_1 - c_{12} s_1 = \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2 - \theta_1) = \sin\theta_2$

Therefore: $\det(J) = L_1 L_2 \sin\theta_2$

</details>

#### Singular Configurations

The singularity condition $\det(J) = 0$ occurs when $\sin\theta_2 = 0$, which means $\theta_2 = 0°$ or $\theta_2 = 180°$.

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Fully Extended (theta2 = 0)">

**Configuration:** The two links lie on one straight line and point the same way.

**Physical meaning:** The end-effector is at the largest reach, $r = L_1 + L_2$. Each joint can only swing it sideways, across the arm. It cannot move along the arm, toward the base or away from it.

**Consequence for collaborative robots:** Near this pose, even a slow motion of the hand along the arm needs very high joint speeds. The controller must slow the motion or change the path before the arm is straight.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Fully Folded (theta2 = 180)">

**Configuration:** Link 2 is folded back along link 1, so the end-effector is at the smallest reach, $r = |L_1 - L_2|$.

**Physical meaning:** As in the stretched pose, the hand cannot move along the arm. If $L_1 = L_2$, the hand lies on the base, and joint 1 cannot move it at all.

**Consequence for collaborative robots:** An arm that leaves a folded pose can accelerate suddenly.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

:::note[Singularity and the Determinant Value]
The size $|\det(J)|$ says how close the arm is to a singularity. For this arm it is largest at $\theta_2 = \pm 90°$. Below a chosen threshold, controllers change to a safer formula called damped least squares, which limits the joint speeds and accepts a small error of the hand.
:::

<Card title="Practical Singularity Thresholds" icon="warning">
The value of $|\det(J)|$ depends on the link lengths, so a threshold for one robot does not fit a larger one. A measure with no units is the **condition number** $\kappa$: the speed of the hand in its easiest direction divided by its speed in the hardest direction (defined in the next part). A rule of thumb, to be tuned for each robot:

- $\kappa < 10$: the hand moves well in every direction
- $\kappa \approx 100$: one direction is clearly slower. Begin to add damping.
- $\kappa \approx 1000$: near a singularity. Use damped least squares.
- $\kappa > 10000$: singular in practice. Reduce speed or plan another path.
</Card>

### Manipulability Ellipsoids

One picture shows what the hand can do in a pose. Give the joints a fixed budget of speed: every pair of joint speeds with $\dot{\theta}_1^2 + \dot{\theta}_2^2 \leq 1$. Mark every hand velocity this budget can give. The marks fill an ellipse (in 3D, an ellipsoid), long in the directions where the hand moves easily and thin where it does not. It is called the manipulability ellipsoid.

The axes of the ellipse come from the **singular value decomposition (SVD)** of the Jacobian, which splits any matrix into a turn, a stretch along fixed axes, and another turn:

$$
J = U \Sigma V^T
$$

The matrix $\Sigma$ holds the stretch factors $\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \ldots$, called the singular values, largest first.

**Key properties:**
- The **half-lengths of the axes** are the singular values, and their directions are the columns of $U$
- The **manipulability index** is $w = \sigma_1 \cdot \sigma_2 \cdots = |\det(J)|$ for a square Jacobian. It is proportional to the area of the ellipse.
- The **condition number** $\kappa = \sigma_{\max} / \sigma_{\min}$ compares the long axis to the short one. $\kappa = 1$ is a circle: every direction is equally easy (isotropic).

In the numerical example, $\sigma_1 = 1.90$ and $\sigma_2 = 0.21$. Their product is $0.40 = \det(J)$ ✅, and $\kappa = 1.90 / 0.21 \approx 9$. The hand moves nine times more easily in its best direction than in its worst.

<Card title="Interpreting the Manipulability Ellipsoid" icon="information">
**Large and round:** the hand moves equally well in all directions. This is the best pose for work beside a person.

**Long and thin:** arrange the task so that its motion lies along the long axis.

**Flat:** the arm is near a singularity. Avoid this pose.
</Card>

### Pseudoinverse for Redundant Robots

Put a fingertip on the table and keep it there. You can still move your elbow. Your arm has more joints than the task needs, and a robot of that kind is called redundant. The simplest example is a planar arm with three joints (3R) that controls only the position $(x, y)$. It has many possible sets of joint speeds for one hand velocity, and $J$ is not square, so it has no ordinary inverse.

The Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse $J^{\dagger}$ chooses the set with the smallest joint speeds overall. With $J^T$ the transpose of $J$ (rows and columns exchanged):

$$
J^{\dagger} = J^T (J J^T)^{-1}
$$

:::note[Prefer SVD for Numerical Stability]
This formula loses accuracy near a singularity. Programs find the pseudoinverse from the SVD: in NumPy, call `np.linalg.pinv(J)`.
:::

The other sets of joint speeds differ from this one by motions that leave the hand still, like the elbow in the fingertip example. These motions are called the null space of $J$. All solutions of $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = J \dot{\mathbf{q}}$ are:

$$
\dot{\mathbf{q}} = J^{\dagger} \dot{\mathbf{x}} + (I - J^{\dagger} J) \mathbf{z}
$$

Here $I$ is the identity matrix and $\mathbf{z}$ is any list of joint speeds you would like to have. The factor $(I - J^{\dagger} J)$ keeps only the part of $\mathbf{z}$ that does not move the hand.

**3-Link Planar Robot Example:**

For a 3R robot with link lengths $L_1, L_2, L_3$ and joint angles $\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3$:

$$
x = L_1 c_1 + L_2 c_{12} + L_3 c_{123}
$$

$$
y = L_1 s_1 + L_2 s_{12} + L_3 s_{123}
$$

The Jacobian is $2 \times 3$:

$$
J = \begin{bmatrix} -L_1 s_1 - L_2 s_{12} - L_3 s_{123} & -L_2 s_{12} - L_3 s_{123} & -L_3 s_{123} \\ L_1 c_1 + L_2 c_{12} + L_3 c_{123} & L_2 c_{12} + L_3 c_{123} & L_3 c_{123} \end{bmatrix}
$$

where $c_{123} = \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2 + \theta_3)$ and $s_{123} = \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2 + \theta_3)$.

The arm has $n = 3$ joints and the task has $m = 2$ coordinates, so $n - m = 1$ free motion is left: the arm can change its shape in one way while the end-effector stays still. Through its choice of $\mathbf{z}$, the controller uses this freedom for a second aim: to stay away from singularities, from joint limits or from an obstacle.

An example for joint limits: give each joint a cost that grows with its distance from the middle of its range, $H(\mathbf{q}) = \sum_i ((q_i - q_{i,\text{center}}) / (q_{i,\max} - q_{i,\min}))^2$. The slope (gradient) of $H$ points toward higher cost, so the program uses the opposite direction as $\mathbf{z}$:

```python
def joint_limit_gradient(q, q_min, q_max):
    """Gradient of the quadratic centering cost."""
    center = (q_min + q_max) / 2
    span   = (q_max - q_min) / 2
    return 2 * (q - center) / span**2

def redundant_ik_velocity(J, dx_desired, q, q_min, q_max, k_sec=1.0):
    """Primary: track dx_desired. Secondary: center joints in their limits."""
    J_pinv = np.linalg.pinv(J)
    z      = -k_sec * joint_limit_gradient(q, q_min, q_max)
    null   = (np.eye(len(q)) - J_pinv @ J) @ z
    return J_pinv @ dx_desired + null
```

The end-effector velocity does not change, because the second term contains only motions that leave the hand still. Keep the gain $k_{\text{sec}}$ small, so that this second aim does not use most of the joint speed.

### Force and Torque Mapping

The same matrix connects forces. Suppose the arm is still and the end-effector pushes on something with a force $\mathbf{F}$, in newtons. Each motor must hold a torque, and $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ is the list of these joint torques, in N·m. The rule comes from power: torque times joint speed at the joints equals force times velocity at the hand, if friction is ignored. With $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = J \dot{\mathbf{q}}$ this gives:

$$
\boldsymbol{\tau} = J^T(\mathbf{q}) \, \mathbf{F}
$$

Velocity goes from the joints to the hand through $J$. Force goes from the hand back to the joints through the transpose $J^T$.

<Card title="Force Mapping for Collaborative Safety" icon="warning">
**Keeping below the force limit:**

To find the force at the hand from measured joint torques, invert the rule. With the largest allowed force $F_{\max} = 150$ N, the controller must keep:

$$\|J^{-T} \boldsymbol{\tau}\| \leq F_{\max}$$

Here $J^{-T}$ is the inverse of $J^T$, and the double bars mean the size of the force. Near a singularity, a small joint torque can give a large force in the direction the arm cannot move. A straight leg works the same way: with the knee locked it holds your weight with almost no effort from the muscles.
</Card>

:::note[Duality Between Velocity and Force]
A direction in which the hand can move fast is a direction in which it can push only weakly, as with the long end of a lever. No pose gives both high speed and high force in one direction.
:::

### Python Code: Jacobian Computation and Velocity Mapping

The first program repeats the numerical example. It prints the same Jacobian, a hand velocity of (-2.187, 1.621) m/s with a speed of 2.722 m/s, $\det(J) = 0.4000$, and joint speeds of 0.259 and -1.143 rad/s for the horizontal motion.

```python
import numpy as np

def jacobian_2r(theta1, theta2, L1, L2):
    """
    Compute the Jacobian matrix for a 2R planar robot.

    Parameters:
        theta1, theta2 : joint angles in radians
        L1, L2         : link lengths in meters

    Returns:
        J : 2x2 Jacobian matrix
    """
    s1 = np.sin(theta1)
    c1 = np.cos(theta1)
    s12 = np.sin(theta1 + theta2)
    c12 = np.cos(theta1 + theta2)

    J = np.array([
        [-L1*s1 - L2*s12,  -L2*s12],
        [ L1*c1 + L2*c12,   L2*c12]
    ])
    return J

def forward_velocity(J, dq):
    """
    Forward velocity mapping: joint velocities -> end-effector velocity.

    Parameters:
        J  : Jacobian matrix (m x n)
        dq : joint velocity vector (n,)

    Returns:
        dx : end-effector velocity vector (m,)
    """
    return J @ dq

def inverse_velocity(J, dx):
    """
    Inverse velocity mapping: end-effector velocity -> joint velocities.
    Uses direct inverse for square Jacobian, pseudoinverse otherwise.

    Parameters:
        J  : Jacobian matrix (m x n)
        dx : desired end-effector velocity (m,)

    Returns:
        dq : required joint velocities (n,)
    """
    if J.shape[0] == J.shape[1]:
        # Square Jacobian: use direct inverse
        det_J = np.linalg.det(J)
        if abs(det_J) < 1e-6:
            print(f"WARNING: Near singularity! det(J) = {det_J:.6f}")
            # Use damped least squares instead
            lam = 0.01  # damping factor
            dq = J.T @ np.linalg.solve(J @ J.T + lam**2 * np.eye(J.shape[0]), dx)
            return dq
        return np.linalg.solve(J, dx)
    else:
        # Redundant robot: use pseudoinverse (minimum-norm solution)
        return np.linalg.pinv(J) @ dx

# ---- Numerical example from the lesson ----
L1, L2 = 1.0, 0.8  # link lengths (m)
theta1 = np.radians(45)
theta2 = np.radians(30)
dq = np.array([2.0, -1.0])  # joint velocities (rad/s)

# Compute Jacobian
J = jacobian_2r(theta1, theta2, L1, L2)
print("Jacobian matrix:")
print(np.round(J, 4))

# Forward velocity mapping
dx = forward_velocity(J, dq)
print(f"\nEnd-effector velocity: dx = {dx[0]:.3f} m/s, dy = {dx[1]:.3f} m/s")
print(f"End-effector speed: {np.linalg.norm(dx):.3f} m/s")

# Determinant (singularity measure)
det_J = np.linalg.det(J)
print(f"\ndet(J) = {det_J:.4f}")
print(f"Analytical check: L1*L2*sin(theta2) = {L1*L2*np.sin(theta2):.4f}")

# Inverse velocity: desired horizontal motion
dx_desired = np.array([0.5, 0.0])
dq_required = inverse_velocity(J, dx_desired)
print(f"\nFor dx = {dx_desired}:")
print(f"Required joint velocities: dq1 = {dq_required[0]:.3f} rad/s, "
      f"dq2 = {dq_required[1]:.3f} rad/s")

# Verify by forward mapping
dx_check = forward_velocity(J, dq_required)
print(f"Verification: dx = {dx_check[0]:.3f}, dy = {dx_check[1]:.3f}")
```

### Python Code: Straight-Line Motion Using the Jacobian

The fourth program moves the hand along a straight line from (1.2, 0.5) m to (0.4, 1.0) m in 2 seconds. At each time step it finds the joint speeds with the inverse Jacobian, then moves the joints by speed times time step (resolved-rate control). The hand speeds up at the start and slows down at the end.

Two points help when you read it:

- It prints a "tracking error" of about 77 mm at most. That number compares the hand with a point that moves along the line at constant speed, so nearly all of it is a difference in timing. The hand stays within about 1.3 mm of the line.
- The option `elbow_down=True` in `ik_2r` selects $\theta_2 > 0$. For the start point this puts the elbow below the line from base to hand, which is the elbow-down pose.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def jacobian_2r(theta1, theta2, L1, L2):
    """Compute Jacobian for 2R robot."""
    s1, c1 = np.sin(theta1), np.cos(theta1)
    s12, c12 = np.sin(theta1 + theta2), np.cos(theta1 + theta2)
    return np.array([
        [-L1*s1 - L2*s12, -L2*s12],
        [ L1*c1 + L2*c12,  L2*c12]
    ])

def fk_2r(theta1, theta2, L1, L2):
    """Forward kinematics for 2R robot."""
    x = L1*np.cos(theta1) + L2*np.cos(theta1 + theta2)
    y = L1*np.sin(theta1) + L2*np.sin(theta1 + theta2)
    return np.array([x, y])

def straight_line_trajectory(L1, L2, q_start, p_start, p_end,
                              duration=2.0, dt=0.01):
    """
    Generate joint trajectories for straight-line end-effector motion
    using resolved-rate (Jacobian inverse) control.

    Parameters:
        L1, L2   : link lengths
        q_start  : initial joint angles [theta1, theta2] in radians
        p_start  : start position [x, y]
        p_end    : end position [x, y]
        duration : motion time in seconds
        dt       : time step in seconds

    Returns:
        t_hist, q_hist, p_hist, det_hist : time-series data
    """
    n_steps = int(duration / dt)
    t_hist = np.zeros(n_steps)
    q_hist = np.zeros((n_steps, 2))
    p_hist = np.zeros((n_steps, 2))
    det_hist = np.zeros(n_steps)

    q = q_start.copy()

    for i in range(n_steps):
        t = i * dt
        t_hist[i] = t
        q_hist[i] = q

        # Current end-effector position
        p_current = fk_2r(q[0], q[1], L1, L2)
        p_hist[i] = p_current

        # Desired end-effector velocity (linear interpolation)
        # Use trapezoidal velocity profile for smooth motion
        s = t / duration  # normalized time [0, 1]
        if s < 0.2:
            # Acceleration phase
            v_scale = s / 0.2
        elif s > 0.8:
            # Deceleration phase
            v_scale = (1.0 - s) / 0.2
        else:
            # Constant velocity phase
            v_scale = 1.0

        # Desired velocity direction and magnitude
        direction = p_end - p_start
        dist = np.linalg.norm(direction)
        # Average speed adjusted for trapezoidal profile
        v_avg = dist / duration
        v_peak = v_avg / 0.8  # compensate for accel/decel phases
        dx_desired = (direction / dist) * v_peak * v_scale

        # Compute Jacobian
        J = jacobian_2r(q[0], q[1], L1, L2)
        det_J = np.linalg.det(J)
        det_hist[i] = det_J

        # Inverse velocity mapping with damped least-squares near singularity
        if abs(det_J) < 0.05:
            # Damped least-squares (Levenberg-Marquardt)
            lam = 0.01
            dq = J.T @ np.linalg.solve(J @ J.T + lam**2 * np.eye(2), dx_desired)
        else:
            dq = np.linalg.solve(J, dx_desired)

        # Clamp joint velocities for safety
        max_dq = 5.0  # rad/s limit
        if np.max(np.abs(dq)) > max_dq:
            dq = dq * max_dq / np.max(np.abs(dq))

        # Integrate joint angles (Euler method)
        q = q + dq * dt

    return t_hist, q_hist, p_hist, det_hist

    r2 = x**2 + y**2
    cos_t2 = (r2 - L1**2 - L2**2) / (2 * L1 * L2)
    cos_t2 = np.clip(cos_t2, -1, 1)

    else:
        t2 = np.arctan2(-np.sqrt(1 - cos_t2**2), cos_t2)
    t1 = np.arctan2(y, x) - np.arctan2(L2*np.sin(t2), L1 + L2*np.cos(t2))
    return np.array([t1, t2])

q_start = ik_2r(p_start[0], p_start[1], L1, L2, elbow_down=True)
```

## System Application: Collaborative Robot Force Limiting

<InArticleAd />


The collaborative robot must follow the path of the handover and keep the force at the end-effector below 150 N. This loop runs in the robot's own controller, many times each second.

### Force-Limited Trajectory Execution

At each time step the controller does this:

<Steps>

1. **Measure** the joint angles $\mathbf{q}$ with the encoders and the joint torques $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ with the torque sensors.

2. **Compute the Jacobian** $J(\mathbf{q})$ for the present pose.

3. **Estimate the force at the end-effector** with $\mathbf{F} = J^{-T} \boldsymbol{\tau}$. If its size is above $F_{\max}$, reduce all joint torques by the same factor.

4. **Check for a singularity** with $|\det(J)|$. If it is below the threshold, change to damped least squares and reduce speed.

5. **Compute the joint speeds** for the wanted hand velocity: $\dot{\mathbf{q}} = J^{-1} \dot{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{desired}}$ (the pseudoinverse for a redundant robot).

6. **Limit the joint speeds** to what the motors and the safety rules allow.

7. **Send** the limited joint speeds to the motor drives.

</Steps>

### Force Monitoring Example

Take the 2R arm in the pose of the numerical example ($\theta_1 = 45°$, $\theta_2 = 30°$), with measured joint torques $\tau_1 = 10$ N·m and $\tau_2 = 5$ N·m. The force at the end-effector is:

$$
\mathbf{F} = J^{-T} \boldsymbol{\tau} = (J^{-1})^T \boldsymbol{\tau}
$$

Take the inverse Jacobian from the numerical example and exchange its rows and columns:

$$
J^{-1} = \frac{1}{0.4000} \begin{bmatrix} 0.2071 & 0.7727 \\ -0.9142 & -1.4798 \end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
(J^{-1})^T = \frac{1}{0.4000} \begin{bmatrix} 0.2071 & -0.9142 \\ 0.7727 & -1.4798 \end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
\mathbf{F} = \frac{1}{0.4000} \begin{bmatrix} 0.2071 & -0.9142 \\ 0.7727 & -1.4798 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -6.25 \\ 0.82 \end{bmatrix} \text{ N}
$$

Row by row:

$$
F_x = \frac{0.2071 \times 10 - 0.9142 \times 5}{0.4000} = \frac{-2.500}{0.4000} = -6.25 \text{ N}
$$

$$
F_y = \frac{0.7727 \times 10 - 1.4798 \times 5}{0.4000} = \frac{0.328}{0.4000} = 0.82 \text{ N}
$$

The size of the force is $\|\mathbf{F}\| = \sqrt{6.25^2 + 0.82^2} = 6.30$ N, far below the 150 N limit in this pose ✅

:::note[Near-Singularity Force Amplification]
As the arm stretches ($\theta_2 \to 0°$), $\det(J)$ falls to zero and the entries of $J^{-T}$ grow without limit. The same two torques give 6.3 N at $\theta_2 = 30°$, 9.1 N at $10°$, 15.4 N at $5°$ and 71.8 N at $1°$. Watching for singularities is a matter of safety.
:::

## Design Guidelines

<InArticleAd />


<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Jacobian Computation" icon="seti:gear">
    Derive the Jacobian as a formula, then program it. Check the program against small test moves of each joint. Compute it again in every control cycle (typically 1 kHz for collaborative robots).
  </Card>
  <Card title="Singularity Management" icon="warning">
    Watch $|\det(J)|$ or the smallest singular value. Near a singularity, use damped least squares ($\lambda = 0.01$ to $0.1$) and reduce speed.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Redundancy Exploitation" icon="puzzle">
    Use the null space for a second aim: joint limits, singularities or obstacles. The motion of the end-effector always comes first.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Force Safety" icon="rocket">
    Use $\boldsymbol{\tau} = J^T \mathbf{F}$ to turn the force limit into joint torque limits, and update them in every control cycle, because $J$ changes with the pose.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

### Practical Checklist

<Steps>

1. **Derive the Jacobian** for your robot and check it with small test moves.

2. **Find the singular poses** in your workspace and keep planned paths away from them.

3. **Set singularity thresholds** from the speed and force your task needs.

4. **Add damped least squares** for poses near a singularity.

5. **Test the force mapping** in several poses, most of all near the edge of the workspace.

</Steps>

:::tip[Industry Best Practice]
Plan paths that stay well inside the workspace, where the hand moves easily in every direction. A path along the edge, or close to a singularity, gains a little reach and loses control and safety margin.
:::

## Summary

### Key Concepts Covered

1. **The Jacobian matrix** turns joint speeds into the velocity of the end-effector: $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = J \dot{\mathbf{q}}$. Each column is the motion of the hand for one joint alone.
2. **Forward velocity mapping** is a multiplication and works in every pose
3. **Inverse velocity mapping** needs $J^{-1}$ or $J^{\dagger}$ and fails at singularities
4. **Singularities** are the poses with $\det(J) = 0$. For the 2R arm, $\det(J) = L_1 L_2 \sin\theta_2$: the arm is singular when straight or fully folded.
5. **Manipulability ellipsoids** show in which directions the hand moves easily
6. **The pseudoinverse** gives a redundant robot the smallest joint speeds, and the null space leaves freedom for a second aim
7. **Force mapping** uses the transpose: $\boldsymbol{\tau} = J^T \mathbf{F}$. Where the hand is fast it is weak.
8. **Safety beside people** needs the force at the hand checked in every control cycle
