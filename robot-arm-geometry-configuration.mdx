## Real-World Engineering Challenge: Industrial Pick-and-Place Cell

<InArticleAd />


An electronics factory wants a robot to move circuit boards between three stations: the conveyor that brings boards out of the solder oven, an inspection camera on a raised platform, and a packaging tray at a lower level. The robot has 4 seconds for each board, and the whole cell (the robot with its stations) must fit on a floor area of 1.2 m by 1.2 m. The team must choose a robot type that reaches all three stations and is fast enough.

:::note[Critical Design Questions]
- How far from the robot base is each station, and what shape of region must the hand cover?
- Which robot type fits the floor area and the reach?
- How do the limits of each joint reduce the region the hand can use?
- What is the smallest number of independent motions the task needs?
- How do the link lengths change the reach?
:::

### Why This Problem Matters for Configuration Selection

The wrong robot type is an expensive mistake, because it is found after the robot is installed. An arm with six joints looks like the safe choice, but for this cell it would cost more and move more slowly than a simpler arm. The task itself tells you which type to choose.

## Fundamental Theory: Robot Arm Structure

<InArticleAd />


### Links and Joints: The Building Blocks

In your own arm, the upper arm and the forearm are stiff parts, and the shoulder and the elbow let them turn. A robot arm is built the same way. The stiff parts are called links, and the moving connections between them are called joints. The first link is the base, fixed to the floor. The last link carries the end-effector, the tool at the end of the arm: a gripper, a welding torch or a sensor. This lesson also calls it the hand.

Each joint gives the arm one or more degrees of freedom (DOF). One degree of freedom is one independent way to move, such as one turn or one slide.

<Card title="Link Parameters" icon="document">
Every joint turns about, or slides along, a line called its axis. Two numbers describe the link between two neighbouring joint axes:

- **Link length** ($a_i$, in metres): the length of the shortest line that joins the two axes. That line is called the common normal.
- **Link twist** ($\alpha_i$, in degrees or radians): the angle between the two axes, seen when you look along the common normal.

In a flat arm all the joint axes are parallel, so every twist is zero and only the lengths matter.
</Card>

:::note[Two Flavors of DH Exist]
Robot makers publish these numbers in Denavit-Hartenberg (DH) tables, and two versions of the method are in use: classical and modified. A number with the same name can mean a different length or angle in each. [Forward and Inverse Kinematics](/education/robotics/forward-inverse-kinematics/) explains the method and the difference.
:::

### Joint Types

A joint allows some motions between two links and blocks all the others. The number that says where a joint is at this moment (an angle or a distance) is called the joint variable. For the full method of counting degrees of freedom, see [Kinematic Joints and Degrees of Freedom](/education/spatial-mechanics/kinematic-joints-degrees-freedom/).

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Revolute (R)">

**Motion:** Turning about one axis, as a door turns on its hinge

**DOF contributed:** 1

**Joint variable:** Angle $\theta$ (radians or degrees)

**Characteristics:**
- Most common joint type in industrial robots
- Compact and stiff
- Driven directly by a rotary motor

**Workspace contribution:** One revolute joint moves the hand along an arc. Two revolute joints in a row let the hand cover a ring-shaped (annular) region in the plane.

The joint cannot turn without limit. Its angle stays between a smallest and a largest value:

$$\theta_{\min} \leq \theta \leq \theta_{\max}$$

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Prismatic (P)">

**Motion:** Sliding along one axis, as a drawer slides on its rails

**DOF contributed:** 1

**Joint variable:** Displacement $d$ (meters or millimeters)

**Characteristics:**
- Used in Cartesian and gantry robots
- High load capacity along the axis of motion
- Needs a linear actuator (ball screw, pneumatic cylinder or linear motor)

**Workspace contribution:** Each prismatic joint extends the workspace in a straight line along its axis, between the two ends of its travel:

$$d_{\min} \leq d \leq d_{\max}$$

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Spherical (S)">

**Motion:** Turning about three axes at right angles that meet in one point, as your shoulder does

**DOF contributed:** 3

**Characteristics:**
- Equivalent to three revolute joints whose axes meet in one point
- Found in the wrist of industrial robots
- Points the tool in any direction
- Hard to build

**Workspace contribution:** A spherical joint does not move the end-effector to a new place. It only changes the direction the tool points.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Other Types">

**Cylindrical (C):** Rotation + translation along the same axis (2 DOF)

**Planar (E):** Translation in two directions + rotation normal to the plane (3 DOF)

**Screw (H):** Turning and sliding along the same axis, tied together as in a bolt in a nut (1 DOF). The slide $d$ equals the pitch $h$ (the advance for each radian of turn) times the angle $\theta$:

$$d = h \cdot \theta$$

**Fixed:** No relative motion (0 DOF). Used for rigid connections and mounting.

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

### Degrees of Freedom

The degrees of freedom of an arm are the number of independent motions it can make at the same time. In a serial arm, where the links follow one another in a single open chain, you add the degrees of freedom of the joints. With $n$ joints, where joint $i$ gives $f_i$ degrees of freedom:

$$\text{DOF} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i$$

A SCARA arm, for example, has three revolute joints and one prismatic joint: $1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 4$ DOF.

To put a tool at any place and point it in any direction in 3D space takes 6 DOF: 3 for the position $(x, y, z)$ and 3 for the direction (roll, pitch and yaw, the three turns of a ship or an aircraft).

<CardGrid>
<Card title="Fewer than 6 DOF">
The arm cannot reach every position with every tool direction. It is enough for work in a plane, or for work that needs position only.

**Examples:** 2R planar arm (2 DOF), SCARA (4 DOF)
</Card>
<Card title="Exactly 6 DOF">
The arm can reach any position inside its range with any tool direction that its joints allow. This is the smallest number for general work in 3D.

**Examples:** Standard 6R industrial arms (KUKA, ABB, FANUC)
</Card>
<Card title="Redundant (more than 6 DOF)">
The arm has more DOF than the task needs. It can use the extra ones to keep its elbow away from an obstacle while the tool stays in place.

**Examples:** 7-DOF collaborative robots, dual-arm systems
</Card>
</CardGrid>

### Link Lengths and Their Effects

The link lengths set the size and the shape of the region the hand can reach. Longer links reach further, but they bend more and carry less. Shorter links are stiffer and more exact, but they cover less space.

The simplest case is a planar 2R arm: two links that move in one flat plane, joined by two revolute joints. Call the link lengths $l_1$ and $l_2$, in metres. The hand is furthest from the base when the arm is stretched straight, and nearest when link 2 is folded back along link 1:

```text
  Stretched: the hand is as far away as it can go

  base o=============o=========> hand
       |<--- l1 ---->|<-- l2 -->|
       |<--- r_max = l1 + l2 -->|

  Folded: the hand is as near as it can come

  base o=============o
          hand <=====+
       |<->|  r_min = |l1 - l2|
```

- **Maximum reach:** $r_{\max} = l_1 + l_2$
- **Minimum reach:** $r_{\min} = |l_1 - l_2|$ (the bars mean "the difference, taken as a positive number")
- **Workspace shape:** a ring (annulus) between the radius $r_{\min}$ and the radius $r_{\max}$

With $l_1 = 0.5$ m and $l_2 = 0.3$ m, $r_{\max} = 0.5 + 0.3 = 0.8$ m and $r_{\min} = 0.5 - 0.3 = 0.2$ m. The hand cannot come closer to the base than 0.2 m, so a hole is left around the base. When $l_1 = l_2$ the hole closes and the workspace is a full disk.

### Workspace Analysis: Reachable vs. Dexterous

<Card title="Workspace Definitions" icon="document">
- **Reachable workspace:** every point the end-effector can reach in at least one tool direction. This is the whole region the robot can touch.
- **Dexterous workspace:** the part of the reachable workspace where the end-effector can also point in any direction. It is never larger than the reachable workspace.
- **Void zone:** a region inside the outer edge that the robot still cannot reach, because of the link lengths, the joint limits, or parts of the robot that would hit each other.
</Card>

:::note[Why dexterous workspace matters]
A robot may take a board from straight above and place it into a tray at an angle. At a point that is reachable but not dexterous, it reaches the tray and cannot hold the board at that angle.
:::

### Joint Limits and Their Impact

A real joint cannot turn or slide without end. Mechanical stops, cables and safety rules limit every joint variable. These limits cut the ideal ring to a smaller region, often with an odd shape.

For a 2R arm where $\theta_1$ (the angle of link 1) and $\theta_2$ (the angle of link 2 measured from link 1) each have a smallest and a largest value:

- If $\theta_1$ can turn a full 360°, the workspace is the same all the way round the base
- If $\theta_1$ is limited (for example to $\pm$150°), the workspace is a sector of the ring, like a cake with a slice removed
- If $\theta_2$ is limited, the arm can no longer fold fully, and the hole around the base grows

## Common Robot Configurations

<InArticleAd />


A configuration is the order of the joint types in an arm and the way its links are arranged. Industry uses four standard ones. Each is written as a row of letters, R for revolute and P for prismatic, starting at the base.

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="SCARA">

### SCARA (Selective Compliance Assembly Robot Arm)

**Joint sequence:** R-R-P-R (two revolute for planar motion, one prismatic for vertical, one revolute for end-effector rotation)

**DOF:** 4

**Workspace shape:** Cylindrical sector (fan-shaped in the horizontal plane, with vertical reach from the prismatic joint)

**Key characteristics:**
- Very fast horizontal motion
- Stiff in the vertical direction, so it can press a part straight down
- Gives a little in the horizontal plane, so a part that is slightly out of line can still slide into its hole (this is the "selective compliance" of the name)
- Small floor area

**Typical applications:**
- PCB component insertion
- Small parts assembly
- Pick-and-place in electronics manufacturing
- Screw driving

**Workspace formula (horizontal plane):**

$$r_{\min} = |l_1 - l_2|, \quad r_{\max} = l_1 + l_2$$

with vertical stroke $d_{\min} \leq z \leq d_{\max}$ from the prismatic joint.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Articulated (6R)">

### Articulated Robot (6-DOF)

**Joint sequence:** R-R-R-R-R-R (six revolute joints, typically grouped as 3 for position + 3 for orientation)

**DOF:** 6

**Workspace shape:** Irregular sphere-like volume (depends on link lengths and joint limits)

**Key characteristics:**
- Most versatile configuration
- Can reach around obstacles
- Large workspace relative to footprint
- Complex kinematics and control

**Typical applications:**
- Welding
- Painting
- Material handling
- Machine tending
- General-purpose manufacturing

**Joint grouping:**
- Joints 1, 2, 3: "Arm" (positions the wrist center in space)
- Joints 4, 5, 6: "Wrist" (orients the end-effector)

In most of these arms the last three joint axes meet in one point, so the wrist acts as a spherical joint.

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Delta">

### Delta (Parallel) Robot

**Structure:** Three or four kinematic chains connect the base platform to the end-effector platform in parallel

**DOF:** 3 (position only) or 4 (position + rotation)

**Workspace shape:** Dome-shaped or cylindrical volume below the base

**Key characteristics:**
- Extremely fast (low moving mass since actuators are on the base)
- High acceleration capability
- Limited workspace volume
- Primarily vertical orientation of end-effector

**Typical applications:**
- High-speed pick-and-place (food, pharmaceuticals)
- Packaging lines
- Sorting operations
- 3D printing (some designs)

**Speed advantage:** The heavy motors stay on the fixed base and only light links move. A delta robot can therefore accelerate at 10 g (ten times the acceleration of gravity) or more, with cycle times under 0.5 seconds.

:::note[Parallel vs Serial Kinematics]
The other three types are serial: one open chain from base to hand. A delta robot is a **parallel mechanism**: several chains join the base to the hand and form closed loops. The rule "add the DOF of the joints" does not work for closed loops: a delta has many joints but only 3 DOF, because most of its joints have no motor and only follow.
:::

</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Cartesian / Gantry">

### Cartesian (Gantry) Robot

**Joint sequence:** P-P-P (three prismatic joints along X, Y, Z axes), sometimes with additional revolute joints for orientation

**DOF:** 3 to 6

**Workspace shape:** Rectangular prism (box-shaped)

**Key characteristics:**
- Simplest kinematics (joint space = Cartesian space)
- Very high rigidity and payload capacity
- Large workspace possible
- Easy to program and control
- Large physical footprint

**Typical applications:**
- CNC machines
- Large-scale material handling
- Palletizing
- Overhead gantry systems in warehouses
- 3D printers (most common configuration)

**Simple position calculation:** Each prismatic joint moves the hand along one axis and nothing else. The position of the hand is the three joint displacements $d_1$, $d_2$ and $d_3$, with no sines or cosines:

$$x = d_1, \quad y = d_2, \quad z = d_3$$

</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Python Workspace Visualization

<InArticleAd />


A program can draw the workspace of a planar 2R arm. It needs one rule, called forward kinematics: where is the hand for a given pair of joint angles? The program applies it to many pairs of angles and marks every point.

### Forward Kinematics of a 2R Planar Arm

Walk from the base to the hand. Link 1 points at the angle $\theta_1$, measured counterclockwise from the $x$ axis. Link 2 is turned by $\theta_2$ from the direction of link 1, so it points at $\theta_1 + \theta_2$. A link of length $l$ that points at an angle $\phi$ moves you $l \cos\phi$ along $x$ and $l \sin\phi$ along $y$. Add the two links to get the hand position $(x, y)$, in metres:

$$x = l_1 \cos(\theta_1) + l_2 \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$$

$$y = l_1 \sin(\theta_1) + l_2 \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$$

Take $l_1 = 0.30$ m, $l_2 = 0.27$ m, $\theta_1 = 54°$ and $\theta_2 = 53°$. Link 2 points at $54° + 53° = 107°$.

$$x = 0.30 \cos 54° + 0.27 \cos 107° = 0.176 - 0.079 = 0.097 \text{ m}$$

$$y = 0.30 \sin 54° + 0.27 \sin 107° = 0.243 + 0.258 = 0.501 \text{ m}$$

The hand is 0.097 m to the right of the base and 0.501 m in front of it. This arm and these angles return when we solve the factory cell.

### Workspace Boundary Computation

The first program draws three workspaces side by side. Expect a full disk of radius 0.8 m for equal links, a ring with a hole of radius 0.2 m for links of 0.5 m and 0.3 m, and a sector with a hole when the joints are limited.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.patches import Circle

def compute_2r_workspace(l1, l2, theta1_range, theta2_range, n_points=200):
    """
    Compute the workspace of a 2R planar robot arm.

    Parameters
    ----------
    l1 : float
        Length of link 1 (meters)
    l2 : float
        Length of link 2 (meters)
    theta1_range : tuple
        (min, max) angles for joint 1 in degrees
    theta2_range : tuple
        (min, max) angles for joint 2 in degrees
    n_points : int
        Number of sample points per joint

    Returns
    -------
    x, y : ndarray
        End-effector positions for all joint combinations
    """
    # Create joint angle grids
    theta1 = np.linspace(
        np.radians(theta1_range[0]),
        np.radians(theta1_range[1]),
        n_points
    )
    theta2 = np.linspace(
        np.radians(theta2_range[0]),
        np.radians(theta2_range[1]),
        n_points
    )

    # Meshgrid for all combinations
    T1, T2 = np.meshgrid(theta1, theta2)

    # Forward kinematics
    x = l1 * np.cos(T1) + l2 * np.cos(T1 + T2)
    y = l1 * np.sin(T1) + l2 * np.sin(T1 + T2)

    return x.flatten(), y.flatten()

def plot_workspace_comparison():
    """
    Compare workspaces for different link length ratios and joint limits.
    """
    fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(15, 5))

    # Configuration 1: Equal link lengths, full rotation
    x1, y1 = compute_2r_workspace(
        l1=0.4, l2=0.4,
        theta1_range=(0, 360),
        theta2_range=(0, 360)
    )
    axes[0].scatter(x1, y1, s=0.1, alpha=0.3, c='steelblue')
    axes[0].set_title('Equal links (0.4, 0.4)\nFull rotation')
    axes[0].set_aspect('equal')
    axes[0].set_xlabel('x (m)')
    axes[0].set_ylabel('y (m)')
    axes[0].grid(True, alpha=0.3)
    # Mark the origin (base)
    axes[0].plot(0, 0, 'ko', markersize=8)

    # Configuration 2: Unequal link lengths, full rotation
    x2, y2 = compute_2r_workspace(
        l1=0.5, l2=0.3,
        theta1_range=(0, 360),
        theta2_range=(0, 360)
    )
    axes[1].scatter(x2, y2, s=0.1, alpha=0.3, c='coral')
    axes[1].set_title('Unequal links (0.5, 0.3)\nFull rotation')
    axes[1].set_aspect('equal')
    axes[1].set_xlabel('x (m)')
    axes[1].set_ylabel('y (m)')
    axes[1].grid(True, alpha=0.3)
    axes[1].plot(0, 0, 'ko', markersize=8)
    # Show the inner void
    void_circle = Circle((0, 0), 0.2, fill=False,
                          linestyle='--', color='red', linewidth=1.5)
    axes[1].add_patch(void_circle)
    axes[1].annotate('Void zone\nr = |l1 - l2|', xy=(0.14, 0.14),
                     fontsize=8, color='red')

    # Configuration 3: Equal links, limited joints
    x3, y3 = compute_2r_workspace(
        l1=0.4, l2=0.4,
        theta1_range=(-150, 150),
        theta2_range=(-135, 135)
    )
    axes[2].scatter(x3, y3, s=0.1, alpha=0.3, c='forestgreen')
    axes[2].set_title('Equal links (0.4, 0.4)\nLimited joints')
    axes[2].set_aspect('equal')
    axes[2].set_xlabel('x (m)')
    axes[2].set_ylabel('y (m)')
    axes[2].grid(True, alpha=0.3)
    axes[2].plot(0, 0, 'ko', markersize=8)

    plt.tight_layout()
    plt.savefig('2r_workspace_comparison.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
    plt.show()

# Run the visualization
plot_workspace_comparison()
```

### Visualizing a Single Arm Configuration

The second program draws one arm in five poses over its workspace. It uses `compute_2r_workspace` from the first program, so run them in the same file or session.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def draw_2r_arm(l1, l2, theta1_deg, theta2_deg, ax, color='steelblue'):
    """
    Draw a 2R planar arm at a specific configuration.

    Parameters
    ----------
    l1, l2 : float
        Link lengths
    theta1_deg, theta2_deg : float
        Joint angles in degrees
    ax : matplotlib axis
        Axis to draw on
    color : str
        Color for the arm links
    """
    theta1 = np.radians(theta1_deg)
    theta2 = np.radians(theta2_deg)

    # Joint positions
    base = np.array([0, 0])
    elbow = np.array([l1 * np.cos(theta1),
                      l1 * np.sin(theta1)])
    end_effector = np.array([
        l1 * np.cos(theta1) + l2 * np.cos(theta1 + theta2),
        l1 * np.sin(theta1) + l2 * np.sin(theta1 + theta2)
    ])

    # Draw links
    ax.plot([base[0], elbow[0]], [base[1], elbow[1]],
            '-o', color=color, linewidth=3, markersize=8,
            markerfacecolor='white', markeredgecolor=color,
            markeredgewidth=2)
    ax.plot([elbow[0], end_effector[0]], [elbow[1], end_effector[1]],
            '-o', color=color, linewidth=3, markersize=8,
            markerfacecolor='white', markeredgecolor=color,
            markeredgewidth=2)

    # Mark end-effector
    ax.plot(end_effector[0], end_effector[1], 's',
            color='red', markersize=10)

    # Mark base
    ax.plot(base[0], base[1], 'ko', markersize=12)

    return end_effector

def show_multiple_configurations():
    """
    Show the same 2R arm in several configurations to illustrate
    how joint angles map to end-effector positions.
    """
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 1, figsize=(8, 8))

    l1, l2 = 0.4, 0.35

    # Draw workspace background
    x_ws, y_ws = compute_2r_workspace(
        l1, l2,
        theta1_range=(0, 360),
        theta2_range=(0, 360),
        n_points=300
    )
    ax.scatter(x_ws, y_ws, s=0.05, alpha=0.1, c='lightgray')

    # Draw arm in several configurations
    configs = [
        (30, 45, 'steelblue'),
        (90, -60, 'coral'),
        (150, 90, 'forestgreen'),
        (210, -30, 'purple'),
        (-45, 120, 'orange'),
    ]

    for theta1, theta2, color in configs:
        end = draw_2r_arm(l1, l2, theta1, theta2, ax, color=color)
        ax.annotate(
            f'({theta1}\u00b0, {theta2}\u00b0)',
            xy=end, fontsize=7,
            textcoords="offset points", xytext=(8, 8)
        )

    ax.set_xlim(-0.9, 0.9)
    ax.set_ylim(-0.9, 0.9)
    ax.set_aspect('equal')
    ax.set_xlabel('x (m)')
    ax.set_ylabel('y (m)')
    ax.set_title('2R Arm: Multiple Configurations in Workspace')
    ax.grid(True, alpha=0.3)

    plt.tight_layout()
    plt.savefig('2r_arm_configurations.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
    plt.show()

show_multiple_configurations()
```

### Comparing Robot Configurations

The third program shows the four workspace shapes from above. It also needs `compute_2r_workspace`. The delta shape is a rough circle, not an exact calculation, and the articulated arm is shown only as its two main links seen from above.

```python
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def scara_workspace_topview(l1, l2, theta1_range, theta2_range, n=200):
    """
    Compute the top-view (XY plane) workspace of a SCARA robot.
    Same as 2R workspace since SCARA horizontal motion is 2R.
    """
    return compute_2r_workspace(l1, l2, theta1_range, theta2_range, n)

def cartesian_workspace_topview(x_range, y_range, n=200):
    """
    Compute the top-view workspace of a Cartesian robot.
    Simply a rectangle.
    """
    x = np.linspace(x_range[0], x_range[1], n)
    y = np.linspace(y_range[0], y_range[1], n)
    X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)
    return X.flatten(), Y.flatten()

def delta_workspace_topview(r_base, r_ee, l_upper, l_lower, n=300):
    """
    Approximate top-view workspace of a delta robot.
    The workspace is roughly a circle centered below the base.

    Parameters
    ----------
    r_base : float
        Radius of the base platform
    r_ee : float
        Radius of the end-effector platform
    l_upper : float
        Length of upper arms
    l_lower : float
        Length of lower arms (parallelogram linkages)
    n : int
        Number of sample angles
    """
    # Approximate effective horizontal reach
    r_max = l_lower * 0.7  # empirical factor for typical delta geometry
    theta = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, n)
    radii = np.linspace(0, r_max, n // 2)
    T, R = np.meshgrid(theta, radii)
    x = R.flatten() * np.cos(T.flatten())
    y = R.flatten() * np.sin(T.flatten())
    return x, y
```

## System Application: Solving the Pick-and-Place Problem

<InArticleAd />


Now return to the circuit board cell. The positions of the three stations tell us how far the hand must reach, and the floor area limits the robot types we can use.

### Station Layout Analysis

The robot base is at the centre of the cell. Each station is marked with its position $(x, y)$ in metres, measured from the base.

```text
  Top view of the 1.2 m x 1.2 m cell (x across, y up)
  0.6 ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
      │                            x Inspection camera │
  0.4 │            x                 (0.10, 0.50)      │
      │      Tray (-0.30, 0.35)                        │
  0.2 │                                 Conveyor x     │
      │                                  (0.45, 0.15)  │
  0.0 │                        o Robot base (0, 0)     │
      │                                                │
 -0.2 │                                                │
      │                                                │
 -0.4 │                                                │
      │                                                │
 -0.6 └────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
     -0.6                     0.0                     0.6
```

<Steps>
1. **Map the station positions**

   Seen from above, the three stations are at about:
   - Conveyor pickup: (0.45, 0.15) m
   - Inspection camera: (0.10, 0.50) m (on a raised platform)
   - Packaging tray: (-0.30, 0.35) m (lower than the robot base)

   All three points must lie inside the reachable workspace.

2. **Calculate required reach**

   The distance of a point $(x, y)$ from the base is $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, by Pythagoras:

   $$r_{\text{conveyor}} = \sqrt{0.45^2 + 0.15^2} \approx 0.47 \text{ m}$$

   $$r_{\text{inspection}} = \sqrt{0.10^2 + 0.50^2} \approx 0.51 \text{ m}$$

   $$r_{\text{tray}} = \sqrt{0.30^2 + 0.35^2} \approx 0.46 \text{ m}$$

   The inspection camera is the farthest station, so the arm must reach at least 0.51 m.

3. **Determine DOF requirements**

   The task needs:
   - Motion across the floor plan, in $x$ and $y$ (2 DOF)
   - Motion up and down to lift and place the boards (1 DOF)
   - A turn about the vertical axis to line up each board (1 DOF)

   The total is 4 DOF. The boards always stay flat, so the tool never has to tilt.

4. **Select configuration**

   The task needs 4 DOF, fast flat motion, a vertical stroke and a small floor area. The **SCARA** configuration gives exactly these.
</Steps>

### SCARA Sizing for the Cell

<details>
<summary>**Link length selection and workspace verification**</summary>

The two horizontal links $l_1$ and $l_2$ of the SCARA must meet two conditions. Stretched, the arm must reach the inspection camera:

$$l_1 + l_2 \geq 0.51 \text{ m}$$

Folded, it must leave only a small hole around the base, so $|l_1 - l_2|$ should be small.

**Selected dimensions:**
- $l_1 = 0.30$ m, $l_2 = 0.27$ m
- $r_{\max} = 0.30 + 0.27 = 0.57$ m. The margin over the farthest station is $(0.57 - 0.51) / 0.51 \approx 12\%$.
- $r_{\min} = 0.30 - 0.27 = 0.03$ m (a small void zone at the base)
- Vertical stroke: 150 mm, enough for the height difference between the conveyor and the tray

**Verification:**
- Conveyor (0.47 m): within reach ($0.47 < 0.57$) ✅
- Inspection (0.51 m): within reach ($0.51 < 0.57$) ✅
- Tray (0.46 m): within reach ($0.46 < 0.57$) ✅
- All stations are outside the void zone (all $> 0.03$ m) ✅

This check uses reach only. A real choice must also check the joint limits from the maker's data sheet.

</details>

### Why Not Other Configurations?

<CardGrid>
<Card title="Articulated 6R">
It has 6 DOF where the task needs 4. It costs more, takes longer to program, and is slower for flat pick-and-place motion.
</Card>
<Card title="Delta">
It is very fast, but its workspace is small. To cover stations 0.5 m from the centre it would need long arms, and long arms lose the speed advantage.
</Card>
<Card title="Cartesian">
It could do the task, but its frame would fill the space above the cell, and it is slower than a SCARA for a cycle of 4 seconds per board.
</Card>
</CardGrid>

## Design Guidelines

<InArticleAd />


### Configuration Selection Rules

<Steps>
1. **Start with the task DOF**

   Count the independent motions the end-effector must make. A position and one angle in a plane need 3 DOF. Any position with any tool direction in 3D needs 6 DOF.

2. **Match workspace shape to station layout**

   Seen from above, SCARA and articulated arms cover a ring, Cartesian robots a rectangle, and delta robots a dome. Choose the shape that covers your stations with the least waste.

3. **Size links using the reach equation**

   For a serial arm, $r_{\max}$ is the sum of the link lengths. Make it at least 10% larger than the distance to the farthest station.

4. **Check the void zone**

   For 2R and SCARA arms, no station may lie inside $r_{\min} = |l_1 - l_2|$.

5. **Verify joint limits do not exclude stations**

   Plot the workspace with the real joint limits. Every station, and the path between stations, must lie inside it.

6. **Consider cycle time and payload**

   Delta and SCARA robots are fast but cover less space. Heavy loads need the stiffer Cartesian or articulated designs.
</Steps>

### Common Design Pitfalls

:::note[Avoid these mistakes]
- **Too many DOF:** a 6-DOF arm for a 3-DOF task costs more and is harder to program.
- **A station in the void zone:** no joint angles reach a station inside $r_{\min}$.
- **No joint limits on the drawing:** a workspace drawn with unlimited joints is larger than the real one.
- **Cables forgotten:** cables and hoses cannot twist without end. Plan their route with the joint ranges.
- **Reachable taken as dexterous:** the hand may reach a point and still be unable to hold the tool at the needed angle.
:::

## Summary

<InArticleAd />


1. **Links and joints** are the parts of every arm. A revolute joint turns, a prismatic joint slides, and each adds one degree of freedom.

2. **Link lengths** set the reach. For a 2R arm, $r_{\max} = l_1 + l_2$ and $r_{\min} = |l_1 - l_2|$. Unequal links leave a hole around the base.

3. **Joint limits** cut the ideal workspace to a smaller real one.

4. **Four standard configurations** cover most industrial work: SCARA, articulated 6R, delta and Cartesian.

5. **Configuration selection** follows from the task: its DOF, the region to cover, the cycle time and the load.
